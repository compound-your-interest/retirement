User Guide

This guide explains every input, every chart, every scenario, and every number in the RetireSmart dashboard. Read it once and you will understand not just how to use the tool — but why each model decision was made.

Overview

The dashboard is a year-by-year retirement projection model that runs from your current age to age 90+. It models six income phases, five types of costs, three asset pools (liquid, retirement accounts, real estate), and up to six market scenarios simultaneously.

Unlike simple retirement calculators, this tool:

  • Models recessions explicitly — not just average returns
  • Separates mortgage (fixed nominal) from living expenses (inflation-adjusted)
  • Models healthcare costs in three phases (employer, ACA, Medicare)
  • Includes a real estate backstop — when liquid assets hit zero, rental equity is tapped
  • Shows dynamic risks and recommendations that change with each scenario
  • Is fully customizable — every number is an input
📌 Who this is for

This tool is designed for people in their 40s–60s with complex financial situations: multiple income streams, equity compensation (RSUs), real estate, deferred compensation, and retirement accounts at multiple employers. If your situation is simpler, many inputs can be set to zero.

Quick Start

1

Open Your Profile

Click "Your Profile" at the top of the dashboard to expand the input panel. All fields have defaults pre-filled. If you are sharing with a friend, click "Reset to Bay Area defaults" first to clear any personal data.

2

Enter Your Numbers

Work through each section: Ages & Timeline, Income Sources, Current Assets, Deferred Comp, Expenses, and Tax Rates. Don't worry about precision — reasonable estimates are fine. The model is most sensitive to retirement age, monthly expenses, and current asset levels.

3

Click Update Dashboard

All five charts, the KPI cards, the risk panel, and the detail table update simultaneously. The model runs a full year-by-year projection from your current age to 90+.

4

Run All Six Scenarios

Click each scenario button — Base, Bull, Bear, Stagflation, 10% correction, 30% crash. Notice how the risks and recommendations change with each scenario. The scenario where your liquid NW hits zero is the most important one to understand.

5

Read the Risk Panel

The Risk Assessment & Recommendations section below the KPIs is scenario-specific and computed from your actual numbers. Red items are critical. Yellow are important. Read these carefully — they represent the most impactful actions you can take.

Deferred Compensation

If your employer offers a Non-Qualified Deferred Compensation (NQDC) plan, enter the details here. The model accrues your deferred comp pre-retirement and pays it out as a lump sum at your elected age, net of the stated tax rate.

🔴 Critical tax planning point

In California, a lump-sum deferred comp payout is taxed as ordinary income at up to 50%+ (37% federal + 13.3% state). If you have a large deferred comp balance, electing installment payouts over 5–10 years can save tens of thousands of dollars in taxes. This is one of the highest-leverage tax decisions available to high-income earners.

Quick Scenarios

Each scenario preset simultaneously adjusts all six market sliders to a coherent set of assumptions representing a macroeconomic environment.

⭐ Base case

20% correction, 3.5% inflation, 7% growth, recession every 9 years. Represents a reasonable planning baseline — neither optimistic nor pessimistic. Start here.

📈 Bull market

10% correction, 3% inflation, 9% growth, recession every 12 years. Optimistic scenario. Good for testing upside potential and whether you can retire earlier.

📉 Bear / recession

30% correction, 4% inflation, 5% growth, recession every 6 years. Conservative scenario. If your plan survives this, it is robust.

🔥 Stagflation

25% correction, 6.5% inflation, 4.5% growth, recession every 7 years. Models a 1970s-style environment — high inflation with low growth. The worst combination for retirement portfolios.

10% correction

Mild correction with base case growth and inflation. Shows how a minor downturn affects your plan. Good for stress-testing without catastrophizing.

30% crash

Severe crash with base case inflation and growth outside of recession periods. Models a 2008 or 2020-style event. Reveals sequence of returns vulnerability.

✅ Best practice

Always run Bear and Stagflation scenarios. If your liquid NW hits zero in either, that is not a prediction — it is a warning that your plan needs adjustment. The Recommended Actions panel will tell you exactly what to change.

Reading the Charts

Chart 1 — Total Net Worth by Age

Stacked bar chart showing three asset pools: liquid portfolio (blue), retirement accounts (green), and real estate equity (purple). Faded colors indicate recession years. This chart shows your total wealth trajectory. Even if liquid hits zero, total NW can remain high — but that means you are forced to liquidate illiquid assets.

Chart 2 — Annual Income by Source

Shows where your money comes from each year. Watch for the cliff when salary stops at retirement and how income rebuilds as 401K draws, Social Security, and rental income take over. The colored bands clearly show which income sources dominate each life phase.

Chart 3 — Annual Costs

Four cost layers stacked: living expenses (red, inflation-growing), mortgage (dark blue, fixed nominal), taxes (amber), and healthcare (orange). The mortgage band disappears completely at payoff age — you will see a step-down in total costs that represents a significant cash flow improvement.

Chart 4 — Liquid NW Health

Color-coded bar chart of your liquid portfolio balance: green = above $500K (healthy), amber = $200K–$500K (watch closely), red = below $200K (danger zone). This is the most important chart for assessing liquidity risk. A good plan keeps this in green or light amber throughout retirement.

Chart 5 — Net Cash Flow

Green bars = surplus years (income exceeds costs — portfolio grows). Red bars = deficit years (costs exceed income — portfolio draws down). In accumulation years, you should see large green bars. In early retirement, red bars are normal if they are modest. Large persistent red bars indicate a structurally underfunded retirement.

The Detail Table

Every year from your current age to 90+ is shown as a row. Red rows are recession years. Yellow rows are years where real estate equity was tapped as a backstop.

Key columns to watch:

  • Net flow — positive means portfolio grows, negative means it draws down. The transition from negative to positive (usually around mortgage payoff age) is a key milestone.
  • Mortgage — shows the fixed $X/mo payment each year, then switches to the green "FREE" label at payoff age.
  • Liquid NW — color-coded green/amber/red. Watch for when this starts declining and at what rate.
  • 401K draw — zero until access age, then a meaningful income stream that grows slightly each year.

KPI Cards

Six summary metrics at a glance:

  • NW at retirement — total net worth at your elected retirement age
  • Liquid at retirement — liquid portfolio only at retirement (most important for near-term sustainability)
  • NW at age 70 — trajectory check at a key milestone
  • Liquid at age 70 — color-coded: green = healthy, amber = watch, red = at risk
  • Cash freed at payoff — annual cash flow improvement when mortgage ends
  • RE backstop used — total real estate equity drawn if liquid depleted. None = plan is self-sustaining. Any amount = you will need to sell property.

Risks & Recommendations Panel

This panel changes with every scenario and is computed from your actual input numbers — not generic advice. Risks are color-coded:

  • 🔴 High severity — requires action before retiring. Ignoring this significantly increases probability of running out of money.
  • 🟡 Medium severity — important to address, but plan survives without action in most scenarios.
  • 🟢 Low severity or opportunity — informational. In bull cases, these may flag upside opportunities (retire earlier, spend more).

Recommendations are sorted by priority (Critical first) and include the specific dollar amounts computed from your inputs — not hypothetical examples.

The Bridge Gap

The bridge gap is the period between your retirement and your 401K access age. During this window, you have no salary, no retirement draws, and (after partner retires) no partner income. Only passive income and the liquid portfolio.

This is almost always the most financially vulnerable period in the entire plan. The longer this gap, the more liquid portfolio you need at retirement. If you retire at 55 and 401K access is 61, you need 6 years of expenses covered from liquid assets alone — typically $1.5–$2.5M depending on lifestyle.

✅ Rule of 55

If you leave your employer at age 55 or later, IRS Rule of 55 allows penalty-free withdrawals from that employer's 401K (not IRAs or other employers' 401Ks). This can dramatically reduce the bridge gap cost. Check with your plan administrator before retiring.

Mortgage Payoff

The model treats your mortgage as a fixed nominal payment that stops completely at payoff age. It does not inflate. This is mathematically correct — your mortgage payment is fixed in nominal dollars. What changes is its purchasing power cost, which declines as inflation rises (another reason inflation helps existing homeowners).

At payoff age, the full monthly mortgage amount disappears from expenses. In the detail table, the Mortgage column switches from a dollar amount to the green FREE label. In Chart 3, the dark blue band disappears. In Chart 5, you will see a step-up in net cash flow. This is one of the most impactful milestone events in the entire retirement plan.

401K Access Age

Standard IRS rules allow penalty-free withdrawals at 59½. Early withdrawals incur a 10% penalty plus ordinary income tax. The model assumes you use 59 as the access age if standard rules apply.

If you elect Rule of 55 (leaving employer at 55+), set this to 55. This dramatically improves bridge phase cash flow. Note: Rule of 55 only applies to the 401K of the employer you leave — not to IRAs or prior employer 401Ks (unless rolled in).

The 401K draw rate in the model starts at 5%/year and increases slightly after age 70 to reflect RMD (Required Minimum Distribution) rules, which force increasing withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts starting at age 73.

Healthcare Costs

Healthcare is modeled in three phases, reflecting how coverage changes through retirement:

  • Before partner retirement: Employer plan (zero ACA cost assumed — partner's employer covers both)
  • Partner retirement to age 65: ACA marketplace — per-person amount × number of people uninsured (1 or 2)
  • Age 65+: Medicare — per-person amount × 2 (both on Medicare)

All healthcare costs are inflation-adjusted. Medical inflation historically runs 5–7%/yr — potentially higher than the general inflation rate you set. The ACA cost per person is a major swing factor. In California, high-income retirees do not qualify for ACA subsidies, so $800–$3,200/month per person is realistic depending on the plan level chosen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the model use a fixed ORCL/RSU price rather than projecting growth?

Conservative planning uses today's price to avoid double-counting. If you want to model price appreciation, you can adjust the RSU price slider upward. The base assumption — that the stock price stays flat — is actually a meaningful hedge: if it grows, your outcome is better than shown.

Why is the mortgage not inflation-adjusted?

Because mortgages are fixed nominal contracts. Your payment is $X/month regardless of inflation. What changes is how much of a burden that payment represents in real terms (it gets easier as inflation erodes its real cost). The model correctly represents this by keeping the mortgage fixed while inflating living expenses.

What does "real estate backstop" mean?

If your liquid portfolio hits zero in any year, the model automatically draws from real estate equity — simulating selling a rental property or drawing a HELOC. The model applies a 25% transaction cost (taxes, realtor fees, time). This means you still have access to wealth, but at a discount. The "RE backstop used" KPI shows total equity drawn.

Should I be worried if the Bear scenario shows me running out of money?

Not necessarily. The Bear scenario is a stress test, not a prediction. Its purpose is to show you the specific ages and amounts where the plan becomes fragile — so you can take preventive action now. The Recommended Actions panel will show you the specific levers (retire later, consult part-time, reduce bridge expenses) and their dollar impact.

The numbers seem better / worse than I expected. What might be wrong?

The most common input errors: (1) entering expenses without mortgage when the model expects the total including mortgage — this understates costs significantly; (2) entering RSU shares as pre-tax shares — the model expects post-tax shares already; (3) setting partner income to zero when partner still works. Double-check these three if results seem off.

Can I use this for a simpler situation (no RSUs, no deferred comp, no real estate)?

Absolutely. Set RSU shares to 0, deferred comp % to 0, rental income to 0, and real estate equity to 0. The model will still run the full projection using salary, savings, retirement accounts, and Social Security only. This represents a more typical single-income household situation.
